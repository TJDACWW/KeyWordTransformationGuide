Overview

Active and Passive Voice

The event stays the same. The grammar only changes the focus.

In active voice, the doer is the subject. In passive voice, the receiver of the action becomes the subject.

Use this guide to decide whether you want to foreground the doer, the action, the result, or the person or thing affected by the action.

Chapter 1

What Active and Passive Mean

Active voice

The subject does the action.

Structure: Subject + verb + object

The chef cooked the meal.

Passive voice

The subject receives the action.

Structure: Subject + be + past participle (+ by + agent)

The meal was cooked by the chef.

The event is the same; the difference is the sentence focus and the order of the words.

Chapter 2

How to Form the Passive

The core steps

  1. Identify the subject, verb, and object in the active sentence.
  2. Make the old object the new subject.
  3. Choose the correct form of be for the tense.
  4. Add the past participle of the main verb.
  5. Add by + agent if you want to name the doer.

Active: Subject + verb + object

Passive: Subject (old object) + be + V3 (+ by + agent)

The chef cooked the meal. → The meal was cooked by the chef.

Chapter 3

Passive Across Common Tenses

The tense stays the same, but the passive moves that tense onto be. The main verb stays in the past participle.

Active tense Active example Passive form Passive example
Present simpleThey make cars.am/is/are + V3Cars are made.
Past simpleThey made cars.was/were + V3Cars were made.
Present continuousThey are making cars.am/is/are being + V3Cars are being made.
Past continuousThey were making cars.was/were being + V3Cars were being made.
Present perfectThey have made cars.has/have been + V3Cars have been made.
Past perfectThey had made cars.had been + V3Cars had been made.
Future with willThey will make cars.will be + V3Cars will be made.
Chapter 4

When to Use Active Voice

Active voice is usually clearer, shorter, and more direct, so it is the default in most everyday, academic, and narrative writing.

  • Use active when the doer is important and you want to highlight them.
  • Use active for lively, engaging writing such as stories, essays, and emails.
  • Use active for instructions and requests when it sounds natural.

Researchers discovered a new species. The committee rejected the proposal. Please fill in the form.

Chapter 5

When to Use Passive Voice

Use passive when the receiver or the action matters more than the doer, or when the doer is unknown, obvious, irrelevant, or sensitive.

  1. Doer unknown or irrelevant.
  2. Doer obvious or not worth repeating.
  3. Focus on result or process.
  4. Formal, scientific, or official style.
  5. To avoid blaming or naming someone.

Her wallet was stolen. Insulin was discovered in 1921. Mistakes were made.

Chapter 6

Be-Passive vs Get-Passive

Be-passive

be + V3

Neutral and common in written or academic English.

My phone was stolen.

Get-passive

get + V3

More informal, often spoken, and often suggests a change or event.

My phone got stolen. She got promoted last year.

Use get-passive only with action verbs. It is not natural with stative meanings such as know or believe.

Chapter 7

True Passive vs Stative Be + V3

Sometimes be + past participle describes a state rather than an event. That is often called a stative passive.

Event passive

Focuses on what happened.

The window was broken by a boy.

Stative result

Describes the current condition.

The window is broken.

The stative reading behaves more like a descriptive state, similar to be + adjective.

Chapter 8

Quick Checklist

  1. What is more important, the doer or what happened?
  2. Is the doer unknown, obvious, or sensitive?
  3. Is the text formal, scientific, or official?
  4. Would passive make the sentence clearer, or would active be shorter and more direct?

Use active by default unless you have a reason to choose passive.
